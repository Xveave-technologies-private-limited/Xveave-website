What to send business users before a fit-to-standard workshop

By Vinod Kumar · 21 November 2026

Send them the standard process in plain language, a picture of it, the decisions you need from them, and a short list of specific questions. Send it a week ahead and ask for answers two days before the session. A fit-to-standard workshop succeeds or fails on whether the business users in the room are seeing the process for the first time.

Why do fit-to-standard workshops go wrong?

The idea is sound: show how standard SAP runs a process, and let the business say where it fits and where it does not. The trouble is what happens when people meet that process cold.

The first hour goes on explaining it. Reactions are instinctive, and the most common one is “we do it differently”, which may or may not be true and cannot be checked in the room. Requirements arrive as wishes, because nobody has had time to think about what they need as opposed to what they have today. And the decisions the workshop was called to make are deferred, because the person who could make them wants to consult someone first.

None of this is the business users’ fault. They were asked to react to something they had never seen.

What should the pre-workshop pack contain?

Five things, and it should take no more than half an hour to read.

1. The process in plain language. Six to ten steps, each saying who does what. “The warehouse confirms what was picked” works. A transaction code does not.

2. One diagram. The same steps as a picture, with the hand-offs between teams visible. People find their own part of a process faster in a diagram than in a paragraph.

3. The decisions you need, written as proposals. For each one: what you propose, and why. “We propose that rush orders confirm only against stock on hand, because they ship within days and cannot wait for a purchase order.” A proposal with a reason can be confirmed quickly. A bare question invites a debate.

4. Specific questions. Not “what are your requirements?” but questions with a real answer: how many orders a day, how often a delivery arrives short, which customers are handled as exceptions and why. Ask about variants directly, such as which order types, channels or countries behave differently.

5. What you are asking them to bring. The reports they rely on, a sample of each form they send to customers, and the spreadsheet they keep because the current system cannot do something.

What should you leave out?

Almost everything else. The full design document does not belong in the pack, and neither does configuration detail, SAP terminology, or anything about how the system will be built. Scope matters too. If the workshop is about order management, say plainly that pricing and returns have their own sessions, so they are not raised here.

The test for each item is whether a business user can respond to it without help. If they cannot, it belongs in the workshop.

How should you ask for their answers?

Make responding easy and bounded. For each step and each proposed decision, ask for one of four responses:

  • Fits. This is how we work, or how we are content to work.
  • Fits with a variation. The same process, but it behaves differently for some orders, sites or customers.
  • Does not fit. We need something this does not do, and here is the business reason.
  • Not sure. We need to see it or discuss it.

Leave room for a comment on each, and ask for answers two days before the session. That gap is what makes the next step possible.

What do you do with the answers?

Build the agenda from them. Everything marked as a fit by everyone is confirmed in the first five minutes and takes no more workshop time. The variations, the gaps and the “not sure” answers are the agenda, ordered by how much rests on them.

Three things change as a result. The workshop spends its time on the points where people disagree or are uncertain. The facilitator already knows where the difficult conversations are and who needs to be in the room for them. And each gap arrives with a business reason attached, which is the difference between a requirement and a preference.

It also shows early who has not engaged. A process owner who has not responded two days before the workshop is a risk worth knowing about before the session, not during it.

Does this replace the workshop?

No. The pack moves the reading and the first reactions out of the room, so the time together goes on discussion and decisions. In our experience a prepared two-hour workshop settles more than an unprepared full day, and people leave it knowing what was agreed.

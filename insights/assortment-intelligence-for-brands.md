# How brands get the right product into stores they don’t own

A brand that sells through distributors and retail partners cannot plan those stores the way a retailer plans its own. What it does control is which products each customer is able to order. That customer-specific assortment is the brand’s real lever, and getting it right means deciding it on clear criteria, being able to explain it, and applying it where the customer actually orders.

## What does “assortment intelligence” mean?

The term is used for three different things, which is worth knowing before you search for a tool.

For online retailers, it usually means **watching competitors**: tracking which products rival sites carry, add and drop.

For grocery and general retailers, it means **optimizing their own ranges and shelves**: which products each of their stores should stock, and how much space each gets.

For a brand, it means something else: **deciding which of its products each customer’s store should carry**, when the brand owns neither the store nor the shelf. This article is about the third.

## Why is the brand’s problem different?

A retailer planning its own stores has the sales data, owns the space, and can simply decide. A brand has none of those advantages.

**The stores belong to someone else.** The brand can influence what a partner stocks, but the partner places the order.

**The lever is access.** What the brand decides is which products a customer may buy, not what ends up on the shelf. A flagship product offered to every door stops being a flagship.

**Every decision is also a conversation.** When a retailer is not offered a product, the account manager has to say why. “Head office decided” is a poor answer to give a long-standing partner.

**The stakes rise with the product.** The more premium and scarce the product, the more it costs to place it in the wrong store, and the more a partner minds being left out.

## How do most brands handle it today?

Usually with store grades and a great deal of manual work. Each door is graded, perhaps A, B or C, and each product is released to a grade and above. Exceptions are agreed by email and maintained by hand.

This works until the business becomes more varied than the grades. The same fixed grades are applied to every category, although a store that is right for footwear may be wrong for fragrance. Nobody can easily say why a particular store holds its grade. A change of policy is applied first and its effects discovered afterward. And the exceptions accumulate until the grades describe very little.

## What does a better approach look like?

Five steps, each fixing one of those weaknesses.

**Score, don’t just grade.** Rate each store on the criteria that matter to you, such as brand fit and commercial performance, and let those criteria differ by category.

**Derive the assortment from the scores.** Each customer’s accessible assortment follows from how its stores score, with room for the exclusives and overrides that every real business needs.

**Simulate before you commit.** Before changing a threshold or a policy, see which products and which stores gain or lose, and what it means financially.

**Govern every change.** Each change carries its reason and its sign-off, so there is a record behind every decision.

**Learn from each run.** Measure what happened and feed it back, so the next season starts from evidence.

## Why does explanation matter so much?

Because the brand is deciding something about another company’s business. A decision that can be traced to a score and a threshold can be defended to a partner, repeated consistently across similar partners, and reviewed when it is challenged.

It matters for a second reason in selective distribution, where a brand limits which retailers may sell its products. In many markets, and in Europe in particular, a brand operating such a system is expected to apply its criteria objectively and consistently. This is not legal advice, and the rules differ by country, but a brand that can show how each decision was reached is in a far better position than one relying on judgment nobody recorded.

## Where does the decision have to land?

In the systems where orders are taken. An assortment that lives in a spreadsheet is a recommendation. To take effect, the customer-specific assortment has to exist in the ERP system and in whatever the customer orders through, so that what each customer sees when ordering is what was decided for them.
